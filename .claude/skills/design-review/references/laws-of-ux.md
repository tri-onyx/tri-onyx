# Laws of UX Reference

Source: [lawsofux.com](https://lawsofux.com/) by Jon Yablonski (CC BY-NC-ND 4.0)

## Visual & Gestalt Principles

### Aesthetic-Usability Effect
Users perceive aesthetically pleasing design as more usable. A polished interface builds trust and forgiveness for minor usability issues. Look for: consistent styling, visual polish, alignment, whitespace balance.

### Law of Common Region
Elements within a shared bounded area (card, box, background color) are perceived as a group. Look for: are related controls grouped inside a visual container? Do unrelated items accidentally share a region?

### Law of Proximity
Objects near each other are perceived as related. Look for: spacing between related vs. unrelated elements, whether labels are closer to their fields than to adjacent fields, logical grouping via whitespace.

### Law of Pragnanz (Simplicity)
People interpret complex images in the simplest form possible to reduce cognitive effort. Look for: can the layout be parsed at a glance? Are there ambiguous visual structures?

### Law of Similarity
Elements that share visual characteristics (color, shape, size, weight) are perceived as related. Look for: do similar items look similar? Do different items look different? Is there accidental visual similarity between unrelated elements?

### Law of Uniform Connectedness
Elements that are visually connected (lines, arrows, shared background, enclosure) are perceived as more related than elements with no connection. Look for: are related elements visually linked? Are there missing connections between things that belong together?

### Von Restorff Effect (Isolation Effect)
When multiple similar objects are present, the one that differs most is best remembered. Look for: do primary actions stand out from secondary ones? Do important status indicators pop against their surroundings? Is there visual hierarchy?

## Cognitive Principles

### Cognitive Load
The total amount of mental effort required to use an interface. Look for: information density, number of decisions per screen, visual noise, jargon, unexplained abbreviations.

### Chunking
Information broken into smaller, meaningful groups is easier to process and remember. Look for: are long lists grouped? Are form fields organized into logical sections? Is data presented in digestible blocks?

### Miller's Law
The average person can hold 7 (plus or minus 2) items in working memory. Look for: navigation items > 9, ungrouped lists > 9, too many tabs/options visible at once.

### Mental Model
Users bring expectations from similar products. The interface should match these expectations. Look for: does the layout follow conventions from similar tools? Are familiar patterns used for common actions (search, settings, navigation)?

### Working Memory
The cognitive system that temporarily holds information needed for current tasks. Look for: does the user need to remember information from one screen to use on another? Are contextual hints available where needed?

### Choice Overload
People become overwhelmed when presented with too many options, often leading to decision paralysis or poor choices. Look for: too many buttons, settings, or options visible at once without clear hierarchy.

### Selective Attention
Users focus on stimuli relevant to their current goal and filter out the rest. Look for: does the interface support focused work without distraction? Are notifications and secondary elements appropriately restrained?

## Interaction Principles

### Hick's Law
Decision time increases with the number and complexity of choices. Look for: can key actions be found quickly? Are menu items organized to reduce scanning?

### Fitts's Law
The time to reach a target is a function of distance to the target and the target's size. Look for: are primary actions large and close to where attention naturally falls? Are destructive actions small and separated? Are click/tap targets at least 44px?

### Doherty Threshold
Productivity soars when computer and user interact at a pace (<400ms) that ensures neither has to wait on the other. Look for: loading indicators, perceived responsiveness, optimistic UI, skeleton states.

### Jakob's Law
Users prefer your site to work the same way as all the other sites they already know. Look for: does the navigation follow standard patterns? Are buttons, forms, and modals placed where users expect?

### Postel's Law (Robustness Principle)
Be liberal in what you accept, conservative in what you send. Look for: does the system handle input variations gracefully? Are error messages helpful? Is validation flexible where possible?

### Tesler's Law (Conservation of Complexity)
Every system has inherent complexity that cannot be removed. The question is who handles it: the system or the user. Look for: is unavoidable complexity managed by the system rather than dumped on the user?

### Occam's Razor
Among competing hypotheses, the simplest is preferred. Look for: unnecessary elements, features that add complexity without proportional value, decorative elements that confuse rather than clarify.

## Behavioral Principles

### Flow
A mental state of complete immersion in an activity. Look for: does the interface support uninterrupted focus? Are transitions smooth? Do pop-ups or notifications break concentration?

### Goal-Gradient Effect
Motivation increases as people get closer to a goal. Look for: progress indicators, step counters, completion percentages, visual progress toward task completion.

### Peak-End Rule
People judge an experience based on how they felt at the peak (most intense moment) and at the end. Look for: is the completion moment well-designed? Are error states (negative peaks) handled gracefully?

### Serial Position Effect
People best remember the first and last items in a series. Look for: are the most important items positioned first or last in lists, menus, and navigation?

### Zeigarnik Effect
People remember uncompleted tasks better than completed ones. Look for: are incomplete items visually indicated? Do drafts, pending actions, or in-progress work draw the user back?

### Pareto Principle
Roughly 80% of effects come from 20% of causes. Look for: does the design prioritize the features most users actually use? Are power-user features tucked away rather than competing for attention?

### Parkinson's Law
Work expands to fill the time available. Look for: does the UI help users complete tasks efficiently? Are there time sinks or unnecessary steps?

### Paradox of the Active User
Users never read manuals; they start using software immediately. Look for: can users accomplish their primary task without instructions? Are there onboarding hints or empty states that guide first use?
